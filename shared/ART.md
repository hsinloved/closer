# Two-Body Studies

*The design philosophy behind Closer's card art. Written with the canvas-design method: the philosophy first, then the art made from it (`shared/art.js`).*

**Two separate systems of marks, studied where they meet.**

Every composition has two presences. Neither is drawn as a figure. Each is a field of patient, repeated marks: one built from parallel hairlines, the other from evenly spaced points or rings. They are laid out with the reserve of a field notebook, the way a careful observer records something too quiet to photograph. The two fields never blend. They overlap, and only in that shared region does a third thing appear: a calm, solid tone that belongs to neither and could not exist without both. That small lens of colour is the heart of every piece, and everything else is arranged so the eye arrives there last.

Space is generous and deliberate. The forms sit off-centre, at weights that seem casual and are anything but. Each placement is the product of painstaking adjustment until the empty paper around the forms carries as much tension as the forms do. Margins are measured, not left over. Hairline registration marks frame the work like plates in a scientific atlas: crop corners, a fine scale along one edge, a crosshair where a centre was found. They suggest an imaginary discipline that measures closeness, and they are drawn with the same meticulous, master-level care as the forms themselves.

The palette is small and fixed: brick, teal and a pale wheat on warm paper, with near-black reserved for the finest structure. Brick and teal are always the two presences, and wheat is always and only the place where they meet. On dark grounds the same three colours glow quietly, and nothing new is added. Every tone is calibrated against the others until it feels inevitable. Nothing is decorative and nothing is loud.

Rhythm comes from repetition. Lines are evenly spaced to the fraction of a unit, dots fall on true grids, and rings step outward at constant intervals. The variation between pieces lives in the relationships: angle, scale, the distance between the two bodies, how much they share. It never comes from added ornament. A series should read like pages from one long, obsessive study, each plate clearly from the same hand, each plainly the result of countless hours of refinement.

Hierarchy is quiet and absolute. The overlap first, the two fields second, the measuring marks last and faintest. There are no words on the art itself; the card's question is the only text, and the image steps back to give it room. The goal is an object that feels expertly crafted and inexhaustible on long viewing, abstract enough to stay out of the question's way, and precise enough to reward a second look.
